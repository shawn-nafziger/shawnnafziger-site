What does this weather mean?

Not "it was 81°F," but "the 2nd warmest October 28 on record, the warmest since 1963." Every day, set against the history of that same date.

How it's built

01

Two data layers, never mixed

Official NOAA station records answer "record" and "Nth warmest" questions. ERA5-Land reanalysis, the same method everywhere from 1950 to now, answers long-run climate questions. Keeping them apart means a record claim is always an official one.

02

Same-date ranks

The model ranks each day against every year's same calendar date, with percentiles, records and normals. Ties share a rank ("years strictly warmer, plus one"), so a rank always means what it says.

03

Forecasts kept, never overwritten

Hourly National Weather Service forecasts for six US cities are saved as they're issued, so they can be graded later against what actually happened.

04

Stories

Evergreen stories such as Phoenix After Dark: a 90°F night used to be rare in Phoenix, and in 2024 there were 39 of them.

Design decisions

Above and below normal use one diverging scale: teal below, orange above, a neutral midpoint, never a rainbow.

Actual and forecast never look alike. Forecast marks are hollow or dashed, and every value carries its "as of" time.

No gauges. A headline number comes with its context: "88°F, 3rd warmest of 155."
